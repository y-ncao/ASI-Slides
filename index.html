Why Now

AI agents changed the trust boundary.

Core thesis

Once agents started acting in production, the problem was no longer "add more security checks." The platform needed to understand who delegated work, which agent acted, what resource was touched, which credential was used, and how to audit the full chain.

Pressure came from two sides

  • Internal agent teams wanted speed and low developer burden.
  • Enterprise customers wanted control, visibility, and trust.
  • Existing IAM assumed humans and services, not delegated agents.

Trust boundary: inbound invocation, outbound action, and audit evidence.

What must be preserved

  • User identity through enterprise or first-party federation.
  • Agent identity as a first-class actor, not user impersonation.
  • Delegation context propagated across tool and API calls.
  • Credential custody through a vault with scoped release.
  • Policy enforcement on the traffic path, not only in docs.

Judgment

Tradeoffs were about enforcement strength, adoption cost, and platform leverage.

Gateway vs. sidecar / plugin

  • Gateway gave stronger enforcement and centralized evidence.
  • Sidecar/plugin paths reduced adoption friction but were easier to bypass.
  • Managed/high-risk internal traffic used stronger enforcement.
  • Long-tail customer paths needed more flexible integration.

Reusable contract vs. one-off integration

  • Move fast on the ecosystem-specific integration surface.
  • Do not let one integration redefine identity or delegation semantics.
  • Keep credential boundary, policy model, and audit contract reusable.
  • Say no when customer urgency would fragment the foundation.
